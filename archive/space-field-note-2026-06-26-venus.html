Venus

Earth's twin, remade by fire
Friday, June 26, 2026 · ~4 min read

Venus is nearly identical to Earth in size, mass, and composition — close enough that astronomers once called it Earth's twin. But look closer, and the resemblance ends. Venus is a cautionary tale written in sulfuric acid: what happens when a planet's greenhouse effect runs away, transforming a potentially habitable world into a 465°C pressure cooker where the atmosphere weighs as much as a kilometer of ocean.

Section 01

Discovery & Naming

Venus has been a fixture of human skywatching longer than recorded history. Its brilliance — the third-brightest object in Earth's sky after the Sun and Moon — made it a cultural landmark for civilizations everywhere. The Babylonians called it Ishtar, the Greeks named it after Aphrodite (goddess of love and beauty), and the Romans gave us the name we use today. Ancient astronomers recognized it as both the morning star and the evening star, but it took Pythagoras in the 6th century BCE to realize they were the same object. Through early telescopes, Venus appeared as a featureless, brilliant disk — no surface details, no moons, no rings. Its true nature remained hidden beneath a global cloud deck so thick that it would be centuries before anyone saw the ground.

Section 02

Physical Profile

Venus is almost a perfect twin of Earth in bulk properties: 12,104 km in diameter (95% of Earth's), 81.5% of Earth's mass, and a similar bulk composition of silicate rock and iron. But the similarities stop at the crust. A slow, retrograde rotation — one Venusian day lasts 243 Earth days, longer than its year of 225 Earth days — means Venus technically spins backwards compared to most planets. The surface itself is relatively young, geologically speaking, with vast volcanic plains, thousands of volcanoes (including some over 8 km tall), and two continent-sized highlands: Ishtar Terra and Aphrodite Terra. At some point in the last 300-500 million years, Venus appears to have undergone a global resurfacing event — possibly catastrophic, planet-wide volcanism — that erased most of its ancient craters and repaved the entire surface.

Section 03

The Hellish Surface — Runaway Greenhouse Made Real

Venus is the hottest planet in the Solar System, not because it is closest to the Sun (Mercury is), but because its atmosphere is a 92-bar blanket of carbon dioxide with clouds of sulfuric acid. The surface temperature is a uniform 465°C — hot enough to melt lead — day and night, pole to equator. The atmospheric pressure is crushing: 92 times Earth's, equivalent to being 900 meters underwater. The Soviet Venera landers that managed to survive the descent transmitted the only direct images we have of the Venusian surface — a flat, orange-lit landscape of fractured volcanic rock under a sky so thick with CO2 that sunlight filters through as diffuse orange twilight. The longest any lander survived on the surface was just over two hours before its electronics failed. The runaway greenhouse on Venus is not a hypothetical future for Earth — it is a real, complete example of what happens when atmospheric CO2 concentrations cross a catastrophic threshold, trapping heat so efficiently that even the oceans would have boiled away entirely.

Section 04

Exploration History — Robots on Hell's Front Porch

Venus has been visited by more spacecraft than any other planet besides Mars, though most met spectacularly short lives. The Soviet Venera program was the undisputed pioneer: Venera 7 made the first successful landing on another planet in 1970, surviving 23 minutes on the surface. Venera 9 returned the first image from another world's surface in 1975 — a grainy, panoramic view of a rocky, orange landscape. In total, 10 Venera landers successfully touched down, each revealing more about the chemistry and geology. NASA's Magellan mission (1990-1994) used synthetic aperture radar to map 98% of the Venusian surface through the clouds, revealing a world of volcanoes, lava channels, and impact craters at a resolution of 100 meters. More recently, interest has surged: NASA's VERITAS and DAVINCI missions, and ESA's EnVision, are all planned for the 2030s, equipped with next-generation radar and atmospheric probes that could finally tell us whether Venus was once wet and temperate — and what turned it into the hellscape it is today.

Section 05

Why It Captivates Us

Venus is a mirror — distorted and terrifying. It began its life with nearly the same ingredients as Earth, in the same neighborhood, under the same Sun. And it went catastrophically wrong. Understanding Venus means understanding the difference between a living planet and a dead one. It is the most powerful argument for why atmospheric composition matters, why we study exoplanets with care, and why our own planetary thermostat must be handled with wisdom. The question that haunts planetary science is not whether Venus was once habitable — it almost certainly had oceans for hundreds of millions of years — but whether we would recognize the signs of life there if it existed before the planet turned into an oven.
