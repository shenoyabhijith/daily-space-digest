Mars

The red planet that was once blue
Wednesday, July 15, 2026 · ~4 min read

Mars is a cold desert today — a rusty, dusty world with a wisp of an atmosphere and frozen water beneath its surface. But the evidence is overwhelming that Mars was not always this way. Ancient river valleys, dried lake beds, and mineral deposits that can only form in liquid water paint a picture of a wetter, warmer, and potentially habitable early Mars. The question is not whether water existed there — it is whether life ever swam in it.

Section 01

Discovery & Naming

Mars has been observed since prehistoric times, its distinctive reddish hue earning it associations with blood, war, and aggression in virtually every culture. The Babylonians called it Nergal (god of war and pestilence); the Greeks named it Ares; the Romans gave us Mars. When Galileo first pointed his telescope at Mars in 1610, he saw not a perfect disk but a gibbous phase — proof that Mars orbits the Sun beyond Earth. In 1659, Christiaan Huygens sketched the first surface feature: Syrtis Major, a dark region still visible today. The 19th century brought the infamous "canals" of Mars — Giovanni Schiaparelli's 1877 observations of linear surface features (canali, meaning channels) were mistranslated as canals, spawning a century of speculation about a Martian civilization that culminated in H.G. Wells's War of the Worlds and an enduring cultural obsession with little green men.

Section 02

Physical Profile

Mars is about half the diameter of Earth (6,779 km) but has only 38% of its gravity and a thin atmosphere that is 95% carbon dioxide. Its surface area is about equal to Earth's dry land — a world of continents without oceans. The planet is divided into two dramatically different hemispheres: the southern highlands, ancient, heavily cratered, and rising 1-3 km above the reference level; and the northern lowlands, younger, smoother, and believed to have once been an ocean basin. Mars has the tallest mountain in the Solar System — Olympus Mons, a shield volcano 21.9 km high (2.5 times Everest's height) and 600 km across, roughly the size of Arizona. It also has the longest canyon system: Valles Marineris, stretching 4,000 km — as long as the continental United States — and reaching depths of 7 km. Both are products of Mars's ancient volcanic past, when the planet was still geologically active.

Section 03

The Water Story — Mars Was Blue

The most profound discovery in Martian science is that liquid water once flowed abundantly on its surface. Orbital images reveal dendritic river valleys that meander and branch exactly like terrestrial river systems. Deltas and alluvial fans mark where rivers emptied into standing bodies of water. The Curiosity rover found conglomerate pebbles — river-worn stones — in an ancient stream bed at Gale Crater, and Perseverance is exploring the remains of a river delta in Jezero Crater that once fed a lake 45 km wide. Chemical evidence from the rovers paints a consistent picture: early Mars (3-4 billion years ago) had a thick atmosphere, a global magnetic field, and a hydrological cycle with rain, rivers, lakes, and possibly a northern ocean containing as much water as the Arctic Ocean on Earth. Then, around 3.5 billion years ago, the magnetic field died, the solar wind stripped the atmosphere away, liquid water became unstable at the surface, and Mars froze into the desert we see today. Most of that ancient water is now locked in subsurface ice, polar ice caps, and hydrated minerals.

Section 04

Exploration History — The Most Visited World Beyond Earth

Mars is the most intensively explored planet beyond Earth, with over 50 missions attempted since the 1960s. NASA's Mariner 4 sent back the first close-up images in 1965 — a cratered, Moon-like surface that shattered hopes of canals. The Viking landers (1976) conducted the first in-situ search for life, delivering ambiguous results that are still debated. After a long hiatus, Mars exploration entered a golden age with the Mars Exploration Rovers: Spirit and Opportunity (2004). Opportunity operated for 14.5 years, traversing 45 km and finding definitive evidence that Mars once had liquid water. Curiosity (2012) discovered that Gale Crater once held a lake with all the chemical ingredients for life. Perseverance (2021) is actively caching samples for future return to Earth, while its companion helicopter Ingenuity completed 72 flights over three years — the first powered flight on another world. Meanwhile, China's Tianwen-1 orbited, landed, and deployed a rover in 2021, and India's Mars Orbiter Mission (MOM — launched 2013, nicknamed Mangalyaan) was the first interplanetary mission to succeed on its maiden attempt, costing less than the movie Gravity. The UAE's Hope orbiter (2021) is studying the Martian atmosphere. Together, they form an armada that is systematically dismantling the red planet's mysteries.

Section 05

Why It Captivates Us

Mars is the only planet where we have active robots exploring the surface right now — a proxy human presence on a world that asks the deepest question: were we alone? Mars is the best candidate for past life in the Solar System, the most realistic target for human settlement, and the only planet we can transform (terraform) with technology we can plausibly envision. It is not a destination — it is a next chapter. Every image from the rovers shows a landscape that feels familiar — rocks, sand, hills, a horizon — yet belongs to an entirely different world. Mars is the place where science fiction becomes engineering.
