Asteroid Psyche

A possible exposed planetary core flying through space
Saturday, July 11, 2026 · ~4 min read

Most asteroids are rock or ice. Psyche appears to be neither. It is a 226-km-wide asteroid that scientists believe is almost entirely made of metal — iron and nickel, the same material that forms the cores of planets like Earth. If this is correct, Psyche is not a typical asteroid. It is a stripped planetary core — the exposed heart of a world that was shattered by a cataclysmic collision early in Solar System history. NASA's Psyche mission, launched in 2023, is now en route to find out for certain.

Section 01

Discovery & Naming

Psyche was discovered on March 17, 1852, by Italian astronomer Annibale de Gasparis, who named it after the Greek mythological figure Psyche (a mortal who married Eros and was deified — often symbolized by a butterfly). It was the 16th asteroid discovered, earning the designation 16 Psyche. Even in the 19th century, Psyche stood out: its brightness and distinctive spectrum suggested a metallic composition unlike most other asteroids. By the early 20th century, astronomers suspected Psyche was made largely of iron and nickel — a hypothesis that planetary radar observations in the 1990s strongly confirmed, showing that Psyche's surface is highly reflective for an asteroid, consistent with metal.

Section 02

Physical Profile — A Metal World

Psyche is about 226 km in diameter — roughly the size of Massachusetts — and is one of the most massive asteroids in the main belt, containing about 1% of the total mass of the entire belt. Its surface is thought to be dominated by iron and nickel, possibly with some silicates mixed in. Radar observations suggest Psyche has a high metal content, though whether it is >90% metal (as some models suggest) or more like 30-60% metal mixed with rock remains an open question — one the Psyche mission is designed to answer. Psyche's density is estimated at 3.9-6.0 g/cm³, depending on how much pore space exists, which is markedly higher than typical rocky asteroids (2-3 g/cm³) but lower than pure iron (7.9 g/cm³), suggesting a mix of metal and rock. If Psyche is indeed a planetary core, it would provide a once-in-a-scientific-lifetime opportunity to study material that normally lies hidden thousands of kilometers beneath a planet's crust and mantle. We cannot drill to Earth's core — it is 2,900 km down and at 5,500°C. But Psyche may have brought its core to us.

Section 03

The Impact Breakup Theory

The leading model for Psyche's composition is that it was once the core of a planetesimal — a small, early planet-like body that differentiated into core, mantle, and crust, just like the inner planets. At some point in the early Solar System, this body was struck by one or more massive impacts that stripped away its rocky mantle and crust, leaving only the metal-rich core exposed. The debris from those impacts may have formed the other, smaller members of the Psyche family of asteroids that share a similar orbit. An alternative theory holds that Psyche never differentiated fully and is instead a primordial, undifferentiated body that formed directly from the most metal-rich material in the protoplanetary disk — a kind of primitive metal chondrite writ large.

Section 04

Exploration History

Psyche has never been visited by a spacecraft — all our knowledge comes from telescopic observations, radar, and spectroscopy. This will change dramatically with NASA's Psyche mission (no clever acronym — it's just called Psyche). Launched atop a SpaceX Falcon Heavy on October 13, 2023, the spacecraft is on a trajectory to reach the asteroid in August 2029 after a Mars gravity assist in 2026. Once in orbit, Psyche will spend 21 months studying the asteroid from four progressively lower orbits, using a multispectral imager, a gamma-ray and neutron spectrometer, a magnetometer, and a radio gravity experiment to determine Psyche's composition, structure, magnetic properties, and geological history. The mission is not just about an asteroid — it is about understanding planetary formation. If Psyche is a core, it will teach us how planets form and differentiate. If it is something else, it will force us to rewrite the textbooks on planetary composition.

Section 05

Why It Captivates Us

Psyche is the only known metal world in the Solar System — there is nothing else quite like it. It offers the closest look we will ever get at a planetary core without drilling through thousands of kilometers of rock. It raises the question of whether metal asteroids could be mined for resources in the future, and it forces a fundamental re-examination of how the building blocks of planets form and evolve. Psyche is a reminder that the asteroid belt is not just a collection of rubble — it is a museum of planetary formation, and Psyche is its most mysterious exhibit.
