Moon

Earth's ancient companion and next frontier
Sunday, June 21, 2026 · ~5 min read

The Moon is the most familiar object in the night sky — visible every clear night, the only celestial body whose surface features are visible to the naked eye, and the only world beyond Earth that humans have walked on. But familiarity breeds underestimation. The Moon is not just a pretty light in the sky or a convenient stepping stone. It is a time capsule from the dawn of the Solar System, a geological record of 4.5 billion years of impacts preserved in vacuum, and the only place where humanity has ever set foot beyond our home planet.

Section 01

Discovery & Naming — Always There, Never Understood

The Moon was observed before there were humans to observe it — it has been the dominant celestial object in Earth's night sky for 4.5 billion years. Every human culture that ever existed told stories about the Moon. The ancient Greeks understood that the Moon orbits Earth and that its phases were caused by reflected sunlight. The name "Moon" comes from the Old English mōna, sharing roots with the Latin mensis (month) — a linguistic reminder that the Moon's phases define one of humanity's most basic timekeeping units. Galileo's first telescopic observations in 1609 revealed mountains, craters, and dark plains that he called "maria" (seas) — a name that stuck even though we now know they are vast basalt plains formed by ancient volcanic eruptions. The far side of the Moon — forever hidden from Earth — was first photographed by the Soviet Luna 3 probe in 1959, revealing a completely different landscape: heavily cratered, nearly devoid of the dark maria that cover the near side.

Section 02

Physical Profile — A World of Crust, Mantle, and Core

The Moon is 3,475 km in diameter — about a quarter of Earth's diameter but only 1.2% of its mass. Its surface is divided into two distinct terrains: the light-colored, heavily cratered highlands (anor-thosite, an ancient rock made of feldspar minerals) that make up most of the far side and the southern near side, and the dark, smooth maria (basalt, similar to Earth's oceanic crust) that cover about 31% of the near side. The Moon has a crust 60-100 km thick on the near side and up to 150 km on the far side, a partially molten silicate mantle, and a small, partially liquid iron core about 350 km in radius. Unlike Earth, the Moon has no global magnetic field — its core solidified too quickly and too completely to sustain a dynamo. The surface is covered in regolith, a layer of pulverized rock and dust created by billions of years of meteorite impacts, ranging from fine dust to rock fragments several meters deep.

Section 03

The Giant Impact — How the Moon Was Born

The leading theory for the Moon's formation is the Giant Impact Hypothesis: about 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized protoplanet named Theia collided with the proto-Earth. The impact was so violent that it vaporized Theia and a large chunk of Earth's mantle, throwing a disk of molten rock into orbit around Earth. This debris — mixed from both bodies — gradually coalesced over a few thousand years to form the Moon. The theory explains why the Moon has such a small iron core (Theia's core merged with Earth's, leaving the Moon with mostly mantle material), why the Moon's composition is nearly identical to Earth's mantle, and why the Moon is depleted in volatiles (most boiled away in the impact). Lunar samples returned by Apollo show oxygen isotope ratios identical to Earth's — the smoking gun that the Moon is made of material from both Earth and Theia, mixed together in the aftermath of a planetary collision that shaped both worlds.

Section 04

Exploration History — The Greatest Journey

The Moon is the only world beyond Earth that has been explored by humans in person. The Apollo program (1961-1972) was the most ambitious engineering project in history. Apollo 11 landed on July 20, 1969, and Neil Armstrong became the first human to set foot on another world, followed by Buzz Aldrin. Over six successful landings, 12 humans walked on the lunar surface, returning 382 kg of rock and soil samples that revolutionized planetary science. Apollo 15, 16, and 17 carried the Lunar Roving Vehicle, allowing astronauts to traverse kilometers from the landing site. Apollo 17's Harrison Schmitt — the only geologist to visit another world — made observations that would have been impossible for a remotely operated rover. After Apollo, lunar exploration entered a robotic era. The Soviet Union's Luna 24 returned the last Soviet sample in 1976. NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (2009-present) has mapped the Moon in unprecedented detail, discovering water ice in permanently shadowed polar craters. China's Chang'e program has been the most ambitious modern effort: Chang'e 3 and 4 landed rovers (including the first on the far side in 2019), and Chang'e 5 returned 1.7 kg of fresh lunar samples in 2020 — the first samples returned since 1976. India's Chandrayaan-3 successfully landed near the south pole in 2023, becoming the first mission to touch down in that region. The Moon now has more active missions than any other body besides Earth.

Section 05

Why It Captivates Us

The Moon is our nearest neighbor — 384,400 km away, close enough that light takes just 1.3 seconds to travel there and back. It stabilizes Earth's axial tilt, driving our seasons and moderating our climate. Its tidal pull shapes our oceans and has slowed Earth's rotation over billions of years. It is the only world where humans have ever been, and it is the next destination: NASA's Artemis program aims to return astronauts — including the first woman and the first person of color — to the lunar south pole by the late 2020s, building a sustained presence with a lunar Gateway station and surface habitats. The Moon is not a destination we visited and left. It is where we learned to survive beyond Earth — and where we will practice for Mars.
