Jupiter

The Solar System's gravitational sovereign
Sunday, June 28, 2026 · ~4 min read

Jupiter is a planet that almost became a star. If it had accumulated about 80 times more mass, its core would have ignited hydrogen fusion, and the Solar System would have been a binary star system. Instead, Jupiter became something almost as remarkable: the largest planet, a gas giant 318 times Earth's mass, whose immense gravity shapes the orbits of everything around it — from asteroids to comets to visiting spacecraft.

Section 01

Discovery & Naming

Jupiter has been known since ancient times — the third-brightest natural object in the night sky, named after the king of the Roman gods (Zeus to the Greeks), and the namesake for the thunderbolt-carrying deity. Its slow, stately motion across the celestial sphere announced its planetary nature to every ancient civilization. But the real discovery came in 1610, when Galileo Galilei turned his primitive telescope toward Jupiter and noticed four small points of light dancing around it. These moons — Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto — were the first objects discovered orbiting something other than Earth or the Sun, providing the decisive observational evidence that not everything revolved around Earth. This discovery was the single most important nail in the coffin of the geocentric model and arguably the beginning of modern observational astronomy.

Section 02

Physical Profile — A Giant Without a Surface

Jupiter is huge enough to swallow 1,300 Earths inside its volume. It is composed almost entirely of hydrogen (89.8%) and helium (10.2%) — essentially the same composition as the Sun, but without enough mass to ignite fusion. Descend through Jupiter's cloud tops and you would never hit solid ground. The atmospheric pressure and temperature increase steadily as you fall, and somewhere around 10,000-15,000 km below the cloud tops, the hydrogen becomes so compressed that it transitions into a exotic state: liquid metallic hydrogen — an electrically conductive fluid that generates Jupiter's immense magnetic field, the strongest in the Solar System (20,000 times Earth's). At the very center, buried beneath 60,000 km of hydrogen and helium, lies a compact core of rock and ice, estimated to be 10-20 Earth masses. The Great Red Spot, Jupiter's most iconic feature, is an anticyclonic storm larger than Earth that has been raging for at least 300-400 years — maybe longer — with wind speeds reaching 430 km/h. Recent Juno data shows it is surprisingly shallow, extending only about 300-500 km down.

Section 03

The Moons — A Solar System in Miniature

Jupiter's four Galilean moons are worlds in their own right. Io is the most volcanically active body in the Solar System, with hundreds of volcanoes erupting sulfur and silicate lava across a constantly resurfaced, pizza-colored landscape. The volcanic energy comes from tidal heating — Jupiter's gravity relentlessly squeezes and stretches Io as it orbits. Europa is the opposite: a smooth, icy world crisscrossed by a network of dark cracks and ridges. Beneath that icy crust, a global subsurface ocean of liquid water — estimated to be 60-150 km deep — contains more than twice the water volume of all Earth's oceans combined. It is arguably the most promising place in the Solar System to look for extant extraterrestrial life. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System (bigger than Mercury), the only moon known to generate its own magnetic field, and is thought to have a subsurface ocean sandwiched between layers of ice. Callisto, the most heavily cratered object in the Solar System, preserves a geological record of impacts spanning 4 billion years, its icy crust frozen in time.

Section 04

Exploration History — From Pioneer to Juno

NASA's Pioneer 10 and 11 made the first flybys in 1973-74, revealing Jupiter's intense radiation belts. The Voyager flybys in 1979 discovered volcanic activity on Io and the complex structure of the rings. The Galileo mission (1995-2003) was the first to orbit Jupiter, dropping a probe into the atmosphere that survived 57 minutes and found far less water than expected — a mystery that persisted for 25 years. Juno, in polar orbit since 2016, has revolutionized our understanding of Jupiter's interior. It has revealed that the Great Red Spot extends far deeper than expected (though still shallow relative to the planet), that Jupiter's atmospheric bands extend 3,000 km deep, and that the core is not a compact ball but a "dilute" core — a fuzzy, partially dissolved mixture of heavy elements and metallic hydrogen. Juno's extended mission is now exploring the Galilean moons as well, setting the stage for ESA's JUICE mission (focused on Ganymede) and NASA's Europa Clipper (arriving 2030).

Section 05

Why It Captivates Us

Jupiter is the Solar System's protector and its wildcard. Its enormous gravity attracts and deflects comets and asteroids, shaping the impact history of every inner planet — including Earth. Without Jupiter, the inner Solar System would be bombarded far more frequently. But it also flings objects inward occasionally. Jupiter embodies the physics of scale: a gas giant where storms are continent-sized, where a single moon (Ganymede) would be a planet if it orbited the Sun, and where the question "what lies beneath the clouds?" drove an entire generation of spacecraft engineering. The answer — metallic hydrogen, dilute cores, global oceans — is stranger and richer than anyone imagined.
