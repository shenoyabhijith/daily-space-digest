Comet 67P

The rubber duck from the dawn of time
Wednesday, June 24, 2026 · ~4 min read

Comets are the Solar System's oldest surviving artifacts — frozen leftovers from the cloud of gas and dust that formed the Sun and planets 4.6 billion years ago. Comet 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko is one of the best-studied of these ancient wanderers, thanks to the European Space Agency's Rosetta mission, which did something no spacecraft had ever attempted: it orbited a comet, landed on its surface, and rode along as the comet woke up near the Sun. The result was a revolution in our understanding of these primordial bodies.

Section 01

Discovery & Naming

Comet 67P was discovered in 1969 by Soviet astronomers Klim Churyumov and Svetlana Gerasimenko at the Alma-Ata Observatory (now Almaty, Kazakhstan). Gerasimenko photographed what she assumed was the periodic comet 32P/Comas Solà, but Churyumov noticed a fainter object nearby that didn't match — it was a new comet, later determined to have an orbital period of about 6.45 years. Under the standard naming convention for periodic comets, it was designated 67P/Churyumov-Gerasimenko (67th known periodic comet). Its nickname among the Rosetta team quickly became "67P" or "the rubber duck" — a reference to its unmistakable two-lobed shape revealed when the spacecraft first approached.

Section 02

Physical Profile — The Rubber Duck

67P is a small comet, about 4.1 km at its longest axis, with a mass of about 10 billion tons. The most striking feature is its bilobate shape: two distinct lobes connected by a narrow neck, giving it the appearance of a rubber duck from a certain angle. The larger lobe (the "body") is about 4.1 x 3.2 x 1.6 km; the smaller lobe (the "head") is about 2.5 x 2.0 x 1.6 km. This shape is common among comets visited by spacecraft and is thought to form through gentle, low-velocity collisions between two separate bodies, or through asymmetric sublimation eroding a larger body. The comet's density is only 0.53 g/cm³ — less than half the density of water — confirming that comets are extremely porous, fragile objects. The surface is incredibly dark — blacker than charcoal — because it is coated with a layer of complex organic compounds and dust. When the Rosetta spacecraft arrived in 2014, 67P was still dormant. Over the following months, the team watched in awe as the comet became active, its surface erupting with jets of gas and dust as sunlight warmed the frozen material.

Section 03

Composition — Icy Museums of the Early Solar System

67P's nucleus is a mix of water ice, carbon dioxide ice, carbon monoxide ice, dust, and complex organic molecules (including glycine, the simplest amino acid, and phosphorus — both essential building blocks of life). When the comet approaches the Sun, ice on the surface sublimates directly into gas, creating the characteristic jets that form the comet's coma (a diffuse cloud of gas and dust) and its two tails. The coma can expand to hundreds of thousands of kilometers across, while the dust tail can stretch over 10 million km. Rosetta's analysis of the gas revealed that 67P's water ice has a higher deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio than Earth's water, suggesting that comets like 67P may not have been the primary source of Earth's water — though comets from other regions could have been. The organic chemistry detected by Rosetta was rich and varied, including molecules such as formaldehyde, ammonia, hydrogen cyanide, methyl isocyanate, and sulfur compounds. The presence of prebiotic molecules strengthens the hypothesis that comets delivered some of the raw materials for life to the young Earth.

Section 04

Exploration History — The Most Daring Comet Mission

Rosetta was the European Space Agency's flagship mission, launched in 2004. It took ten years to reach 67P, using gravity assists from Earth (three times) and Mars. On August 6, 2014, Rosetta entered orbit around the comet — the first spacecraft ever to orbit a comet. The orbiter carried a lander called Philae, which detached on November 12, 2014, and attempted the first landing on a comet. The landing was not smooth: Philae's harpoons failed to fire, the lander bounced twice, and came to rest in a shadowy crevice where its solar panels could not generate enough power. Philae operated for about 60 hours on its primary battery, transmitting data from all ten instruments before going silent — but in those 60 hours, it delivered groundbreaking measurements of the comet's surface composition and physical properties. The orbiter continued studying the comet as it approached the Sun, witnessing peak activity in August 2015, then following it back out toward the outer Solar System. The mission ended on September 30, 2016, with a controlled descent and impact on the comet — Rosetta's final transmission confirmed contact with the surface it had studied for over two years.

Section 05

Why It Captivates Us

Comets are time machines. The materials frozen inside 67P have been sealed in deep freeze for 4.6 billion years, unaltered by the heat and pressure that transformed the planets. They contain the primitive organic chemistry from the birth of the Solar System — the same chemistry that may have seeded Earth with the ingredients for life. 67P's rubber-duck shape, its awakening jets, its unexpected organic complexity, and its slightly grudging welcome of Philae make it feel like a character, not just a lump of ice and dust. Every comet that swings past the Sun is carrying a message from the beginning that we are only beginning to decode.
