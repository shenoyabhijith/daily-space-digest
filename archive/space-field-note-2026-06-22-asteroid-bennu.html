Asteroid Bennu

A carbon-rich time capsule from the dawn of the Solar System
Monday, June 22, 2026 · ~4 min read

Bennu is not a planet, not a moon, and not a comet. It is a near-Earth asteroid — a dark, diamond-shaped rubble pile about 500 meters across, tumbling through space between Earth and Mars. But what makes Bennu extraordinary is what it carries: pristine organic material and hydrated minerals from the very earliest days of the Solar System, delivered to Earth by NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission in 2023 — the largest sample return since Apollo.

Section 01

Discovery & Naming

Bennu was discovered on September 11, 1999, by the LINEAR survey telescope in New Mexico. It was designated 1999 RQ36 before being officially numbered 101955. The name Bennu was chosen in 2013 through a student contest — the winner, Michael Puzio, a third-grader from North Carolina, suggested it after the Bennu bird from Egyptian mythology, a heron-like deity associated with creation and the Sun. The name is fitting: the Bennu bird was said to have perched on the primordial mound at the dawn of creation, and the asteroid carries material from the dawn of the Solar System.

Section 02

Physical Profile — A Rubble Pile

Bennu is what planetary scientists call a 'rubble pile' — it is not a single solid rock but a loosely bound aggregation of boulders, pebbles, and dust held together by its own weak gravity. It is shaped like a spinning top (or a diamond) about 492 meters in diameter at its equator, with a rotation period of 4.3 hours. Its density is only 1.19 g/cm³ — barely denser than water — confirming its porous, loosely packed structure. The surface is covered in boulders, some larger than a house, with one (called the 'Sphinx') measuring 18 meters tall. When OSIRIS-REx approached Bennu, it expected a smooth, sandy surface based on ground-based telescopic observations. Instead, it found a brutal, boulder-strewn landscape that made sample collection much harder than anticipated. Bennu is carbon-rich (B-type asteroid), and its composition includes hydrous clay minerals — meaning water was once present in its parent body, chemically bound into the rock.

Section 03

Why Bennu Matters — The Ingredients of Life

Bennu is a primitive, carbonaceous asteroid — a fragment of a much larger body that formed in the early Solar System and never underwent the heating and differentiation that created the planets. This makes it a pristine sample of the building blocks from which the planets (including Earth) were assembled. Its composition includes organic compounds, hydrated minerals, and possibly the precursors of amino acids and nucleobases — the molecules that, on early Earth, may have combined to form the first life. The OSIRIS-REx sample, returned to Earth on September 24, 2023, is the largest extraterrestrial sample retrieved since the Soviet Luna 24 mission in 1976 and the first asteroid sample ever returned by the United States. The 121.6 grams of Bennu material (surpassing the mission's 60-gram goal by 100%) are being analyzed by laboratories worldwide, providing the most direct look yet at the raw ingredients that were available when life emerged on Earth. Early analyses have already revealed the presence of water-bearing clay minerals, carbonates, and organic molecules — exactly the kind of chemistry that may have seeded Earth with the building blocks of life.

Section 04

Exploration History

Bennu is the target of NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission (Origins, Spectral Interpretation, Resource Identification, Security-Regolith Explorer), launched in 2016. The spacecraft reached Bennu in 2018, spent two years mapping it in unprecedented detail, and executed a breathtaking sample collection maneuver on October 20, 2020 — briefly touching the surface with a robotic arm called TAGSAM (Touch-And-Go Sample Acquisition Mechanism), releasing a burst of nitrogen gas to stir up surface material, and collecting far more than expected. The sample return capsule landed in the Utah desert on September 24, 2023. But the mission's work isn't done: the spacecraft, now renamed OSIRIS-APEX, is on its way to study another near-Earth asteroid called Apophis, which will make a close flyby of Earth in 2029 — a remarkable second act for a spacecraft that has already delivered one of the most important scientific payloads in history.

Section 05

Why It Captivates Us

Bennu is both a scientist's dream and a practical concern. Its orbit brings it close to Earth every six years, and there is a small (1-in-2,700) chance of impact in the late 22nd century. More urgently, it represents the kind of asteroid that could one day be mined for water and resources, or that we might need to deflect. But the deepest reason Bennu captivates is what it can tell us about ourselves: the molecules that made life possible on Earth were not created on Earth. They formed in interstellar space, were incorporated into asteroids like Bennu, and delivered to the young Earth by impacts. Bennu's samples may tell us how common — or how rare — the ingredients of life are in the universe.
